# Is Your Battery PFAS-Free? Screening for the EU 2026 Deadline

**How to audit your bill of materials for PFAS substances before regulation takes effect**

## The Clock Is Ticking

The European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) universal PFAS restriction proposal, if adopted, would ban or severely restrict per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances across most industrial applications. For battery manufacturers, semiconductor fabs, and chemical companies, this means auditing every material in your supply chain.

The challenge: PFAS aren't always obvious. PVDF is a fluoropolymer. PTFE is Teflon. Nafion is a perfluorosulfonic acid membrane. Some materials you use every day are PFAS -- and you may not realize it until the ban takes effect.

## Urgency Levels Explained

KOMPOSOS assigns urgency based on current regulatory status:

- **Critical**: Already banned (PFOA, PFOS under Stockholm Convention)
- **High**: Ban effective within 12 months
- **Moderate**: Restricted or proposed for restriction
- **Low**: Under review but no action yet
- **None**: Not PFAS or not restricted

All fluoropolymers currently used in batteries (PVDF, PTFE, FEP) are at **moderate** urgency -- proposed for restriction under the ECHA universal PFAS proposal but not yet banned.

## What Should You Do?

1. **Audit your BOM now**. Run every material through a PFAS check. Even materials that seem safe (LiPF6 contains fluorine but isn't a PFAS polymer) need verification.

2. **Identify high-risk materials**. Any fluoropolymer binder, gasket, or coating is likely PFAS.

3. **Start replacement qualification**. The CMC+SBR transition for battery binders is well-documented (Tesla, BYD, and CATL all use water-based anode processing). Start qualification now, not when the ban hits.

4. **Consider the supply chain**. Your Tier 2 suppliers may also use PFAS in processes you don't control.
